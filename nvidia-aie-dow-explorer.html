01

What this guide is, in one screen

The Department of War is no longer running a handful of AI science projects. It is standing up enterprise platforms that serve millions of users, and simultaneously pushing inference down to ships, aircraft, and command posts. Those two motions need very different infrastructure, and that is the opening for a software platform that runs the same way in both places.

The three patterns worth memorizing

  • Enterprise generative AI is consolidating. Service-run chatbots are folding into one department-wide platform. The differentiated conversation is no longer “which chatbot” but what runs on-premises behind it.
  • Mission AI is decentralizing. Computer vision, sensor fusion, autonomy, and command and control are being pushed to edge and tactical networks where commercial cloud does not reach.
  • Data platforms are multi-vendor by policy. Open DAGIR and the NGC2 common data layer are explicitly designed so no single vendor owns the stack. Interoperability is the requirement, not a nice-to-have.

Where NVIDIA AI Enterprise actually enters the conversation

NVIDIA AI Enterprise is the supported, security-maintained software layer that sits between accelerated hardware and a mission application. A program office rarely buys it because they want “AI software.” They buy it because they have an accreditation deadline, an air-gapped enclave, a model they cannot send to a commercial endpoint, or a sustainment problem with open-source containers nobody owns.

That means your three strongest entry points are an authorization boundary that will not move, a classified or disconnected environment, and a sustainment and patching obligation that an unsupported open-source stack cannot satisfy. Everything in the branch sections below is organized around finding those three.

Where the money is, and whether it can be spent

The fiscal 2027 request is the largest defense budget ever proposed, and it names artificial intelligence infrastructure explicitly. Almost none of that headline money is available to spend today. This section separates what was asked for from what Congress has actually provided, because confusing the two is the fastest way to build a pipeline that evaporates.

The rest of the AI request

Four components of the artificial intelligence and joint command and control request were itemized publicly. They total roughly fifty-seven billion dollars of the fifty-eight and a half billion headline; the remainder is not broken out in the public summaries.

How to read these numbers

  • A request is not an appropriation. The department asked for this. Congress has not provided it. Those are separate events, often months and sometimes years apart.
  • The headline AI and autonomy money is contingent. Most of it is mandatory funding riding on a reconciliation bill, and reconciliation first requires a budget resolution that has not passed.
  • The committees have acted on the base portion only. Against a request of roughly fifty-five billion dollars for the autonomous warfare organization, the House and Senate armed services committees and House appropriators each wrote down about one billion.
  • One phrase is worth memorizing. The request describes prioritizing enterprise-scale AI infrastructure. That is the department, in its own budget language, describing what you sell. Quote it back to them rather than inventing your own framing.
Where fiscal 2027 stands today
Fiscal 2027 begins 1 October 2026. Everything below is the public record as of 20 September 2026.

What a continuing resolution does to your pipeline

  • No new starts. A program office generally cannot begin something new with money provided under a continuing resolution.
  • No multiyear contracts and no production rate increases without a specific exception.
  • Exceptions are rare. They are called anomalies. Across fiscal years 2013 to 2017, thirteen of three hundred eighty-eight anomaly requests made it into legislation.
  • This is normal, not a crisis. Since fiscal year 2000 the department has begun twenty-one of twenty-seven fiscal years under a continuing resolution or a shutdown. It last started a year with full-year appropriations in fiscal 2019.
  • The practical effect on you: until full-year appropriations arrive, money moves through things that already exist. Task orders on vehicles already awarded, existing program lines, and options on current contracts.

What to do between now and 11 December

  • Map every opportunity to a funding line or an existing vehicle. If it maps to neither, it is not fundable this quarter, whatever the customer's enthusiasm.
  • Ask which of their fiscal 2027 plans assume reconciliation. Many program staff have not been told, and the answer tells you whether their timeline is real.
  • Get onto task order teams now. Under a continuing resolution, an award through an existing vehicle is the path of least resistance, and teaming decisions are made before the money moves.
  • Watch three dates. 1 October, when fiscal 2027 begins under the stopgap. 11 December, when the stopgap expires. And whenever a fiscal 2027 budget resolution moves, because that is the gate for everything contingent.
  • Treat reconciliation money as upside, never as forecast. Build the number you commit to on base lines you can point at.

08

Route to market

NVIDIA goes to market through partners. In this customer set that is not a preference, it is a requirement: the paper almost always flows through a reseller or integrator holding the right vehicle, and the software usually lands on somebody else's hardware or platform. Know which lane your deal is in before the first meeting.

Hardware OEMs and certified systems

Who owns the box

NVIDIA publishes a certified-systems program, and the major server manufacturers build accelerated platforms against it. In defense deals the OEM often carries the sustainment contract, the supply chain paperwork, and the relationship with the installation.

Seller move: find out which OEM already has the sustainment contract at that program office before you propose anything. You are joining their deal far more often than they are joining yours.

Government cloud regions and marketplaces

Who owns the environment

Accelerated instances and the software platform are offered through government cloud regions and their marketplaces. This is the fastest path for an unclassified or controlled-unclassified workload where the customer already holds a cloud authorization.

Seller move: ask which cloud the program is already authorized in. A program with an existing authorization in one region will not move for your convenience.

ISVs and platform vendors

Who owns the application

Most of the programs in this guide are delivered as somebody's application or data platform, not as raw infrastructure. Data platforms, mission autonomy software, generative AI workspaces, and container platforms all run on accelerated compute underneath.

Seller move: the ISV is your fastest route to a requirement. If their product needs accelerated inference to hit a performance target, the pull comes from them, not from you.

Resellers and contract vehicles

Who owns the paper

Publicly known routes into this customer set include government-wide acquisition contracts, service-specific IT vehicles, cloud acquisition vehicles, and rapid prototyping authorities used by innovation organizations. Which one applies depends on the buying office, the dollar value, and the urgency.

Seller move: confirm vehicle eligibility with your contracts team early. A vehicle mismatch discovered late is the most common reason a funded defense deal slips a quarter.

The question that saves a quarter

Ask it in the first meeting: “When this gets funded, whose contract does it land on, and is that vehicle already in place?” If nobody in the room can answer, you do not have a deal yet. You have an interested engineer, which is a fine place to start and a bad place to forecast from.

09

Deployment and accreditation, explained plainly

This is the part that trips up sellers coming from commercial accounts. Defense customers cannot run software until an authorizing official signs for the risk. Your job is to make that signature easier to get, and to never claim it on the customer's behalf.

IL2Public / low

Non-critical, publicly releasable information. The easiest environment to stand up a demonstration in, and the least representative of where the mission actually runs.

IL4CUI

Controlled unclassified information. Most enterprise and back-office AI work lives here. This is where a large share of generative AI and analytics programs operate today.

IL5Higher CUI / NSS

Controlled unclassified information with higher sensitivity, and unclassified national security systems. This is the level defense buyers most often name when they say a capability is “real.” Enterprise generative AI for the department has been targeted at this level publicly.

IL6Secret

Classified up to Secret. Fewer offerings exist here, connectivity is constrained, and on-premises or sovereign deployment becomes the default rather than an option.

EdgeDisconnected

Air-gapped, tactical, afloat, or deployed. No commercial endpoint, intermittent bandwidth, and hard size, weight, and power limits. Inference has to happen locally, which is exactly where a portable software stack earns its keep.

Three things to say correctly, every time

  • Software is not authorized; systems are. A product can meet controls and ship hardened builds, but the authorization to operate belongs to the system owner and their authorizing official. Never tell a customer a product “is FedRAMP” or “is IL5.”
  • FedRAMP and impact levels are different ladders. FedRAMP is the civilian-origin cloud authorization program. Impact levels come from the defense cloud computing security requirements guide. Defense customers care about both, for different reasons.
  • Continuous authorization is the goal, not a one-time ATO. Several programs in this guide are explicitly building continuous authorization pipelines. A stack that can be patched and re-attested without breaking the baseline is worth more than one that was compliant on the day it shipped.

What makes an accreditation conversation go well

  • Bring the artifacts, not adjectives. Software bills of materials, vulnerability remediation cadence, long-term support branch policy, and hardened container variants are what a security engineer actually reads.
  • Ask for the boundary diagram. If the customer can show you where their authorization boundary sits, you can tell them in one meeting whether the deployment is straightforward or hard. Most sellers never ask.
  • Find the reciprocity angle. If a sister program already carries an authorization for a similar stack, inheriting controls is dramatically faster than starting over. Ask who else in their service has done this.
